🖼 The Word Dictionary (click any word)

One real-life analogy per term. That is all you need to follow the whole page.
🧑‍🍳 AI AgentA restaurant waiter. You say what you want; he doesn’t just hand you a menu — he walks to the kitchen, checks the chef, brings your food. An agent uses “tools” to finish your request.
🍳 ToolThe waiter’s helpers: the menu (look things up), the kitchen (do work), the phone (call outside). Tools are the things an agent is allowed to use.
💰 Reliable agentA waiter who, when the kitchen says “no fish,” doesn’t freeze — he checks again, offers chicken, then tells the manager. “Reliable” = keeps working when things go wrong.
⏱️ TimeoutLike calling a friend: you don’t wait forever. After ~10 seconds you hang up and try again. Timeout = “stop waiting, move on.”
📞 Retry (with backoff)Calling back, but not 100 times in 1 second. You wait a little longer each time (2s, 4s, 8s) so you don’t spam.
🔃 IdempotencyPressing the elevator button twice doesn’t call two elevators. A safe “already did this” tag, so a retry doesn’t charge the card twice.
📚 RAGA librarian. Instead of memorizing every book, he finds the 3–5 most useful books for your question, reads them, and answers using only those.
🪄 Embedding / Vector DBA magic shelf where books sit by MEANING, not title. “How to fix a bike” lands next to “repairing wheels.” Lets the computer find similar ideas fast.
🃏 ChunkingCutting a thick textbook into small recipe cards, so you grab just the card you need instead of the whole book.
✅ Recall@k (retrieval quality)Did the librarian bring the RIGHT books? If you asked for a chocolate-cake recipe and 0 of his 5 books had cake — bad recall.
🤬 Faithful vs HallucinationFaithful = “the book says X.” Hallucination = the librarian invents “and also Y” that no book ever said.
📝️ EvaluationA test with 100 questions you already know the answers to. You check the librarian’s score BEFORE trusting him with real customers.
💍 OrchestrationA wedding planner coordinating caterer, florist, photographer and band so everything arrives and the day flows.
🎮 State / checkpointA video-game save point. Game crashed at level 3? Reload the save — don’t replay the whole game.
✈️ Saga / compensation (undo)You booked a flight AND a hotel. Hotel is sold out. You don’t strand the person — you cancel the flight too and tell them. “Undo” = compensation.
✍️ Human-in-the-loopBig purchases need a manager’s signature. The robot does the work but stops and asks a human before the risky step.
🚘 Latency / p95How fast the waiter brings food. p95 = “95 out of 100 orders arrived within this time” — the “almost always fast” number.
💵 CostThe bill. Use the cheap chef for toast, the fancy chef only for the wedding cake.
🚗 GuardrailsSeatbelts + airbags. Check what the customer said (guard in) and check the AI’s answer is safe (guard out) before it goes out.
🛯 PII maskingBlurring the home address on a package before a stranger sees it.
🔑 RBACOnly the manager has the safe key. Different people can do different things.
🐦 Canary deployTrying a new recipe on 5 customers first, before changing the whole menu.

Q1 • Building an agent vs making it reliable

Interviewer asks: Your refund tool returns {amount:null}. Does the workflow recover? Are retries and timeouts handled? Walk me through.
🧈 Explain like I’m 5: Picture a restaurant. A basic waiter hears “no fish today” and just stands there frozen. A reliable waiter does four smart things: he waits and asks again (retry), he doesn’t wait all day (timeout), he offers chicken instead (a backup plan), and he remembers the table so a restart doesn’t lose the order (a save point). Your AI agent is that waiter.
🌍 Real world: Gartner (2024) predicted that through 2025, at least 30% of AI projects get abandoned after the demo — almost always because the happy path worked in the demo but broke in real life. Reliability is what separates a demo from a product.

🖼️ Picture: frozen waiter vs smart waiter

🎮 Playground — be the waiter: Send an order. Sometimes the kitchen is busy. Watch what a reliable agent does.
💬 Say it in the interview: “I wrap every tool call with a short timeout and a few retries that wait longer each time, so I never hang or spam. I validate the shape of the answer before trusting it. If it still fails, I fall back to a safe default or a human. I save a checkpoint so a crash resumes instead of restarting, and I trace every run so I can see what happened at 3am.”

Q2 • Building RAG vs evaluating RAG

Interviewer asks: How would you measure retrieval quality? Without evaluation, you’re just hoping it works.
🧈 Explain like I’m 5: A librarian answers your question from books. Before you let him help real customers, you’d test him with questions you already know the answer to. Two things matter: did he bring the right books (recall)? And did he tell you only what the books say (faithful) — or did he make stuff up (a hallucination)? Testing first is called evaluation.
🌍 Real world: In Feb 2024, Air Canada’s chatbot invented a refund policy that didn’t exist. A tribunal ruled the airline had to honor the chatbot’s made-up words. Hallucination isn’t a harmless typo — it created a real legal cost. That’s why we measure faithfulness.

🖼️ Picture: the librarian pipeline

🎮 Playground — test the librarian: Try three questions and see recall & faithfulness change.
💬 Say it in the interview: “I build a golden test set of ~100 real questions with known answers. I score retrieval separately from answering: Recall@5 for ‘did I find the right cards,’ and faithfulness for ‘did the answer stay inside the sources.’ I only ship when both pass a bar — and I keep re-scoring nightly so a bad chunk or a stale price is caught automatically.”

Q3 • Using tools vs orchestrating a workflow

Interviewer asks: The agent must fetch customer data, call an API, validate, and update a system. What if the API fails halfway? Show me state, error recovery, and control.
🧈 Explain like I’m 5: A travel agent books your flight, then tries your hotel — and the hotel is sold out. A careless agent leaves you with a flight to nowhere. A smart agent undoes the flight (refunds it), tells you, and offers options. That “undo” is called compensation. And like a wedding planner, a good agent keeps a checklist (state) so a hiccup doesn’t mean starting over.
🌍 Real world: In 2012 a code bug at Knight Capital executed bad trades and lost $440 million in 45 minutes. The lesson FDEs live by: risky steps need an undo plan, a checkpoint, and a human approval before the dangerous action — not hope.

🖼️ Picture: happy path vs the undo rescue

🎮 Playground — run the trip: Each click is a new customer. Watch the undo plan kick in.
💬 Say it in the interview: “I keep explicit state so I always know which steps are done. Each step that touches the outside world has a matching undo — a saga — so a failure rolls back cleanly instead of leaving a half-done mess. Before any risky write, I pause for human approval. Everything is checkpointed so a crash resumes exactly where it stopped.”

Q4 • Building AI vs solving the customer’s problem

Interviewer asks: An FDE doesn’t just build AI and hand it over. How do you find the real problem and deliver something people use?
🧈 Explain like I’m 5: A gadget seller shows you a shiny robot and asks if you like it. A doctor first watches you walk, asks where it hurts, then fixes the one painful thing first. A Forward Deployed Engineer is the doctor: shadow the customer’s day, find the real pain (say, 40 minutes of typing per case), fix that one thing, show the hours and money saved — then grow from there.
🌍 Real world: Remember that Gartner stat — ~30% of AI projects die after the demo? Almost all of them skipped this step. The teams that win time-study the workflow first, wrap the tools the customer already uses (Salesforce, SAP, email), and ship one thin slice in two weeks before scaling.

🖼️ Picture: doctor, not gadget seller

💬 Say it in the interview: “I’d spend day one shadowing the workflow and timing the steps to find the real bottleneck, not the flashiest one. I’d write the problem in the customer’s words and attach a number to it. Then I’d ship the smallest useful slice against the systems they already use, measure the hours saved, and only scale once that slice earns trust.”

🏆 Quick check (click to reveal)

1. A tool keeps failing. What are your first two moves?

Wait longer between retries (backoff), and set a timeout so you never hang forever. Then a safe fallback.

2. What are the two RAG tests, in plain words?

Did the librarian bring the RIGHT books (recall)? Did the answer stay inside the books (faithful) instead of inventing?

3. Flight booked, hotel sold out. What’s the smart move?

Run the undo plan (compensation): refund the flight, notify the customer, offer options. Don’t strand them.

4. Why “try it on 5 users first”?

That’s a canary deploy — catch problems on a few people before changing it for everyone (avoid a Knight-Capital-style blast radius).

5. What’s the very first step an FDE takes with a new customer?

Shadow the workflow and time the steps to find the real pain — before writing any AI code.
